## 4.1 Data

We use RecIF, the public benchmark released with Kuaishou's OpenOneRec project (Zhou et al., 2025), available on Hugging Face under an Apache-2.0 licence after accepting its terms. Its log file covers 162,074 users and 75,671,142 impressions; for each impression it records whether the user watched to the end ("long view"), liked, followed, shared, or marked the video "not interested". Users' histories are ordered in time, with a median of 493 impressions per user. Each of the 12.7 million videos has a machine-generated text description (median 173 Chinese characters), written in a neutral reporting style, and a three-level semantic ID used by the OneRec recommender. We never see the videos themselves, so every label in this report is a judgement about the description, not the video.

Two samples are drawn from the logs. For the overview in Section 4.2 we drew impressions uniformly from all users' histories, so a video appears in proportion to how often it was shown. For the case study we used videos whose descriptions contain one of 15 marriage-and-family keywords (such as *divorce*, *mother-in-law*, *bride price*, *husband*); 260,566 videos match, and together they make up 2.0% of all impressions. Each user saw a median of 8 of them (10th–90th percentile 3–17), and only 813 users saw 50 or more. Keywords only select candidates: the language model then decides whether each video is actually about the issue.

The data have three limits that shape the analysis. The users are a sample of Kuaishou's general population, not students. Per-user counts on any one issue are small, so individual users' skew cannot be measured from the logs and is studied in simulation instead. And the logs record what users were shown and how they reacted, not what they wanted to see.

## 4.2 Which issues carry opposing narratives

**Method.** Rather than starting from issues we expected to matter, we let the data show which social issues users see. We drew 3,000 impressions uniformly from user histories, so each video appears in proportion to how often it was shown, and had the reference model (DeepSeek V4 Pro) label each description with a cross-domain prompt (Appendix A.6): the domain, the issue, whether the video itself endorses a contested position, and if so which type: *only-right-way* (one choice is the only proper path), *binary opposition* (two opposed camps, nothing between), *anxiety-raising* (fear or shame pushes a choice) or *group label* (a whole group portrayed negatively). Reporting or rebutting a claim, a conflict story without a side, and marketing do not count (Appendix A.1). 2,973 outputs were valid. The open model (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507) labelled the same impressions for comparison.

About 7% of impressions (6.5%–8.4%) touch a social issue, and the five studied domains account for about 4.5% (3.8%–5.3%). Gender and relationships is the largest issue domain; its most frequent issues are dating and break-ups, marriage, and gender roles. Only-right-way is the most common type (16 of the 39 impressions with an opposing narrative).

**A larger, stratified sample.** Each domain in Table 4.1 has only 2 to 54 impressions. To estimate the opposing share within the studied domains more precisely, a cheaper model (deepseek-flash) screened 20,000 further impressions, drawn the same way (19,773 valid). From each studied domain it flagged we drew up to 200 impressions for the reference model to label, weighted each by the number flagged in its domain over the number labelled, and grouped them by the reference model's domain. The screen over-flags: the reference model confirmed only 46%–60% of each flagged domain.

The two samples agree, and the larger one narrows the intervals: about one impression in seven in the studied domains carries an opposing narrative (13%; 10%–17%), from 7% in family and generations to 20% in consumption and money. Gender and relationships carries all four types, led by only-right-way; group labels against a gender are rare there (2 of 128). Domain sizes come from Table 4.1, because the stratified sample covers only what the screen flagged; its 79 initially invalid outputs were transient API failures and all became valid on retry. Typical disputes include how much a partner should spend on dates, strict versus relaxed parenting, and the civil-service exam versus starting work.

**The open model overestimates at both steps.** It placed 22% of the same impressions in a social-issue domain, three times the reference model, and judged 47% of those to carry an opposing narrative. The reference model put 450 of its 660 issue impressions in non-issue content; on the 210 both treated as issue content, the open model labelled 69% as opposing and the reference model 17%. It also had no marketing rule, and at least 10 of its 27 anxiety-raising labels in consumption fell on promotions.

**Case study and exclusions.** We study gender and relationships because it is the largest issue domain and carries all four types. Within it we focus on gender group labels: they are rarer than only-right-way, but the most clearly defined type, so they can be labelled and checked (Appendix A.3), and the most directly harmful, since they disparage a whole group rather than a choice. Their rarity is also the premise of the detection analysis in Section 5. We leave out history, patriotism, policing and surveillance, which are politically sensitive and harder to label and discuss ethically, and domains with too few impressions to say anything.

## 4.3 How reliable the labels are

No video was labelled by hand. To estimate label quality, the open model, the reference model and, on the first 150 videos, a second reference (GPT-6.1 Sol) labelled the same 500 marriage-and-family videos, sampled in proportion to exposure. We ran this check under three prompt versions (Appendix A.4). A larger sample, enriched with every candidate video whose description contains a label word such as *fenghuang nan* or *lao nü*, was also labelled by the reference model to measure the share more precisely.

**Table 4.3.** Gender group labels on the same 500 marriage-and-family videos under three prompt versions, and in the enriched sample. Precision, recall and kappa compare the open model with the reference model; 95% intervals in brackets.

| | v0 | v1 | v2 (final) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Open model: videos flagged | 20 (4.0%) | 252 (51%) | 66 (13%) |
| Reference model: videos flagged | 7 (1.4%) | 3 (0.6%) | 5 (1.0%; 0.4%–2.3%) |
| Second reference (first 150 videos) | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Open model's precision | 10% | 0.8% | 6% (2%–15%) |
| Open model's recall | 2/7 | 2/3 | 4/5 (38%–96%) |
| Cohen's kappa | 0.13 | 0.00 | 0.10 (0.02–0.20) |
| Reference model, enriched sample: exposure-weighted share | 0.8% (0.5%–1.1%) | — | 0.2% (0.1%–0.4%) |

**The reference model is stable; the open model is not.** Across versions the reference model flagged 0.6%–1.4% of videos, and its five flags under v2 are all among its seven under v0; it gave identical labels to the 105 videos that appear in both samples. The open model ranged from 4% to 51%. All final labels therefore come from the reference model, and every share states the model and prompt version.

**Gender group labels are rare.** Depending on the sample, between about 0.2% and 1% of marriage-and-family exposure carries a gender group label. The 500-video check gives 1.0%. The enriched sample, several times larger, gives 0.2% (0.1%–0.4%): videos with a label word take 0.43% of exposure and 11% of that is flagged, while among the other videos the flagged share is 0.17%. Pooling the two samples' videos without a label word gives 0.3% (0.2%–0.6%). Both samples are drawn in proportion to exposure from the same candidates, and the reference model labels the videos they share identically, so the gap reflects how few positives each sample finds.

**The open model over-flags, and the disagreement can be located.** Under v2 it finds four of the reference model's five videos, but only 6% of its own flags are confirmed. Because v2 records the answers to the four questions of Appendix A.3, every one of the 63 disagreements can be traced to the first question, who the target is: the open model tends to read a *topic* (women and money) as a *target* (women). Appendix A.4 gives the details, including how the errors changed across versions.

**Limits.** The reference is a model, not a human gold standard, so errors both models share, such as cues visible only in the video, cannot be detected; and the two strong models disagree on rare borderline videos (each flagged two of the first 150, one in common).

## 4.4 Do opposing narratives attract more engagement?

A common worry is that ranking by engagement amplifies divisive content because people engage with it more (Milli et al., 2025). We checked whether that holds in these logs. For each labelled video we counted, across all users, how often it was shown, watched to the end, liked or marked "not interested", and pooled the rates over impressions. Intervals come from resampling videos, since impressions of the same video are not independent.

**Table 4.4.** Engagement in RecIF by DeepSeek label (95% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of videos).

| Videos | Videos (impressions) | Watched to end | Liked | "Not interested" |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Marriage videos with a gender group label | 144 (3,054) | 27% (17–37%) | 4.7% (1.3–9.0%) | 0.03% (0–0.14%) |
| Other marriage videos | 2,206 (307,921) | 40% (38–42%) | 4.6% (4.3–4.9%) | 0.09% (0.08–0.10%) |
| Issue videos with an opposing narrative (overview sample) | 38 (23,807) | 36% (30–45%) | 5.0% (3.2–7.6%) | 0.09% (0.04–0.15%) |
| Issue videos without one (overview sample) | 179 (88,029) | 40% (35–44%) | 5.3% (4.4–6.3%) | 0.08% (0.06–0.11%) |

We find **no sign that opposing narratives are engaged with more**. Group-label videos are liked at the same rate as other marriage videos and, if anything, watched to the end less often; across issues, videos with and without an opposing narrative have overlapping rates on every measure. Two cautions: most group-label videos come from the keyword stratum of our enlarged sample, so they are not a random draw of such videos; and the logs show reactions to what Kuaishou chose to show, not to all such content. The result means that, in this data, any tilt towards these narratives is unlikely to come simply from users rewarding them with engagement; Section 5 instead examines how an individual preference, if present, would be amplified.
