## 4.1 Data

We use RecIF, the public benchmark released with Kuaishou's OpenOneRec project (Zhou et al., 2025), available on Hugging Face under an Apache-2.0 licence after accepting its terms. Its log file covers 162,074 users and 75,671,142 impressions; for each impression it records whether the user watched to the end ("long view"), liked, followed, shared, or marked the video "not interested". Users' histories are ordered in time, with a median of 493 impressions per user. Each of the 12.7 million videos has a machine-generated text description (median 173 Chinese characters), written in a neutral reporting style, and a three-level semantic ID used by the OneRec recommender. We never see the videos themselves, so every label in this report is a judgement about the description, not the video.

Two samples are drawn from the logs. For the overview in Section 4.2 we drew impressions uniformly from all users' histories, so a video appears in proportion to how often it was shown. For the case study we used videos whose descriptions contain one of 15 marriage-and-family keywords (such as *divorce*, *mother-in-law*, *bride price*, *husband*); 260,566 videos match, and together they make up 2.0% of all impressions. Each user saw a median of 8 of them (10th–90th percentile 3–17), and only 813 users saw 50 or more. Keywords only select candidates: the language model then decides whether each video is actually about the issue.

The data have three limits that shape the analysis. The users are a sample of Kuaishou's general population, not students. Per-user counts on any one issue are small, so individual users' skew cannot be measured from the logs and is studied in simulation instead. And the logs record what users were shown and how they reacted, not what they wanted to see.

## 4.2 Which issues carry opposing narratives

**How the taxonomy was built.** Rather than starting from a list of issues we expected to matter, we let the data show which social issues users actually see. We drew 3,000 impressions uniformly from RecIF user histories, so each video appears in proportion to how often it was shown, and asked the reference model (DeepSeek V4 Pro) to label each one from its text description, using a cross-domain prompt (Appendix A.6): the broad domain, a short name for the specific issue, whether the video itself endorses a contested position, and if so which of four narrative types it uses. The prompt applies the common rules in Appendix A.1: reporting or rebutting a claim, telling a conflict story without taking a side, and marketing do not count. 2,973 outputs were valid; the 23 invalid ones are left out of every share. The four types are:

- **Only-right-way**: one choice is presented as the only proper path, and other choices are belittled.
- **Binary opposition**: a question is framed as having exactly two opposed camps or options.
- **Anxiety-raising**: fear, urgency or shame is used to push a view or a choice.
- **Group label**: a whole social group (by gender, region, age and so on) is portrayed negatively, or a single case is presented as typical of the group.

The model writes the issue name as free text, so for domains with at least 30 impressions we grouped the names into sub-issues with fixed keyword rules applied to the model's own labels. For comparison, the same impressions were also labelled by the open model (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507) with an earlier prompt; its figures are shown in the last column of Table 4.1.

**Where opposing narratives appear, and what kind they are.** Most of what users are shown is not about social issues at all: about 7% of impressions (95% interval 6.5%–8.4%) touch a social issue, and the five domains we study account for about 4.5% (3.8%–5.3%). Within those five domains, roughly one impression in six carries an opposing narrative (17%; 12%–24%). Gender and relationships is the largest issue domain, and about one in five of its impressions carries an opposing narrative; about half of these present one choice as the only right one. In body and appearance the opposing narratives we found are all of the only-right-way kind, about appearance standards. Across the whole sample, only-right-way is the most common type (16 of the 39 impressions with an opposing narrative), followed by anxiety-raising (8), group label (6) and binary opposition (5). Group labels are rare: only one fell in gender and relationships, consistent with the rate of roughly 1% that the reference model gives on a larger sample of marriage videos (Section 4.3).

Three cautions apply. First, each domain has only 2 to 54 impressions, so the intervals are wide, often 20 to 40 percentage points; the table shows where opposing narratives concentrate and what kind they are, not precise levels. Second, the cross-domain prompt excluded marketing, and none of the anxiety-raising labels in consumption fell on promotions; with the open model and no such rule, at least 10 of 27 did. Third, the labels come from a model, checked only against other models (Section 4.3).

**The open model overestimates at both steps.** The open model labelled 22% of the same impressions as social-issue content, three times as many as the reference model, and judged almost half of those (47%) to carry an opposing narrative. Of the 660 impressions it placed in a social-issue domain, the reference model placed 450 in non-issue content. On the 210 impressions both models treated as issue content, the open model labelled 69% as opposing and the reference model 17%. This matches the pattern in Section 4.3: the open model reads drama, jokes and everyday family scenes as stances.

**Typical disputes.** The following are illustrative paraphrases of the kind of argument found in each studied domain, written by us from the issue labels; they are not quotations from videos.

- *Gender and relationships*: whether how much a partner spends on dates shows how much they care; how high a bride price is reasonable; whether a woman should keep her own income after marriage. A group-label version: "men from poor rural families bring their whole family into the marriage".
- *Body and appearance*: whether clear skin, a slim body or greater height is needed to be treated well, and whether there is one right standard of beauty.
- *Family and generations*: strict versus relaxed parenting; how much grown children owe their parents; conflict between a wife and her mother-in-law.
- *Life choices and paths*: taking the civil-service exam versus starting work; "lying flat" versus striving.
- *Consumption and money*: saving versus enjoying life now; whether young people should still buy a home and a car.

**Why gender and relationships is the case study.** It is the largest social-issue domain in what users are shown, and it carries three of the four narrative types. The most common type there is only-right-way. Group labels are rarer, but they are the most clearly defined type, so they can be labelled and checked (Appendix A.3), and the most directly harmful, since they disparage a whole group rather than a choice. Their rarity is also the premise of the detection analysis in Section 5: a narrative that makes up about 1% of a user's issue videos is hard for the user to notice.

**What we left out, and why.** We exclude three groups of content from the main analysis. (1) *History, national memory and patriotism*, which make up a third of the culture-and-values domain (17 of 51 impressions), and *policing, surveillance and campus control* in public order and freedom. These are politically sensitive: we expect labels to disagree more, and both the ethics of studying users' exposure to them and open discussion of results are harder. (2) *Resources and fairness*, *study and ideas* and *other social issues*, each with 9 or fewer impressions. (3) The rest of culture and values, a broad mix of tradition, celebrity and online culture with no common dispute.

## 4.3 How reliable the labels are

No video was labelled by hand. To estimate label quality, three models labelled the same 500 marriage-and-family videos independently, sampled in proportion to exposures: the open model (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507), the reference model used for all final labels (DeepSeek V4 Pro), and, on the first 150 videos, a second reference (GPT-6.1 Sol). We ran this check three times as we revised the prompt (Appendix A.4); Table 4.2 summarises the gender group label, the attribute used in the case study.

**Table 4.2.** Gender group labels on the same 500 marriage-and-family videos under three prompt versions. Precision, recall and kappa compare the open model with the reference model; 95% intervals in brackets.

| | v0 | v1 | v2 (final) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Open model: videos flagged | 20 (4.0%) | 252 (51%) | 66 (13%) |
| Reference model: videos flagged | 7 (1.4%) | 3 (0.6%) | 5 (1.0%; 0.4%–2.3%) |
| Second reference (first 150 videos) | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Open model's precision | 10% | 0.8% | 6% (2%–15%) |
| Open model's recall | 2/7 | 2/3 | 4/5 (38%–96%) |
| Cohen's kappa | 0.13 | 0.00 | 0.10 (0.02–0.20) |

Four findings follow. First, **the reference model is stable and the open model is not.** Across the three versions the reference model flagged between 0.6% and 1.4% of videos, and its five flags under v2 are all among its seven under v0. The open model ranged from 4% to 51%. This is why all final labels come from the reference model, and why every share in this report states the model and prompt version. Second, **gender group labels are rare**: about 1% of marriage-and-family videos by the reference model, the same order as in our pilot. Third, **the open model still over-flags**: under v2 it finds four of the reference model's five videos, but only 6% of its own flags are confirmed, and raw agreement (87%) looks high only because the label is rare.

Fourth, **the disagreements can be located.** Under v2 each model answers the four questions of Appendix A.3 as separate fields, and a label is accepted only if it follows from the answers. In all 63 videos on which the two models' labels differ, their answers already differ at the first question, who the target is: in 55, the open model named a gender group where the reference model saw no target at all; in 7, the reference model saw an individual. Whenever both models named a gender group, they agreed on the remaining questions and on the label. Reading the reasons, the open model tends to treat a *topic* as a *target*: a video about women and money is read as a claim that women care only about money. Under the earlier prompts its errors were of a different kind: a mother-in-law or stepmother treated as a whole gender, a single villain in a drama treated as typical of the group, and teasing treated as disparagement (v0); and, under v1, which asked the model to state the target before its reasoning, a stock opening of "target: group" written for almost every video, including those it then labelled "none".

This check has clear limits. The reference is itself a model, not a human gold standard, and errors shared by both models, such as cues that appear only in the video and not in its description, cannot be detected. The two strong models also disagree on rare borderline videos: each flagged two of the first 150, and only one in common. Because the reference flags so few videos, recall is estimated imprecisely; an enlarged set of reference positives, drawn by screening for cue words, is being labelled to narrow it.

## 4.4 Do opposing narratives attract more engagement?

A common worry is that ranking by engagement amplifies divisive content because people engage with it more (Milli et al., 2025). We checked whether that holds in these logs. For each labelled video we counted, across all users, how often it was shown, watched to the end, liked or marked "not interested", and pooled the rates over impressions. Intervals come from resampling videos, since impressions of the same video are not independent.

**Table 4.3.** Engagement in RecIF by DeepSeek label (95% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of videos).

| Videos | Videos (impressions) | Watched to end | Liked | "Not interested" |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Marriage videos with a gender group label | 144 (3,054) | 27% (17–37%) | 4.7% (1.3–9.0%) | 0.03% (0–0.14%) |
| Other marriage videos | 2,206 (307,921) | 40% (38–42%) | 4.6% (4.3–4.9%) | 0.09% (0.08–0.10%) |
| Issue videos with an opposing narrative (overview sample) | 38 (23,807) | 36% (30–45%) | 5.0% (3.2–7.6%) | 0.09% (0.04–0.15%) |
| Issue videos without one (overview sample) | 179 (88,029) | 40% (35–44%) | 5.3% (4.4–6.3%) | 0.08% (0.06–0.11%) |

We find **no sign that opposing narratives are engaged with more**. Group-label videos are liked at the same rate as other marriage videos and, if anything, watched to the end less often; across issues, videos with and without an opposing narrative have overlapping rates on every measure. Two cautions: most group-label videos come from the keyword stratum of our enlarged sample, so they are not a random draw of such videos; and the logs show reactions to what Kuaishou chose to show, not to all such content. The result means that, in this data, any tilt towards these narratives is unlikely to come simply from users rewarding them with engagement; Section 5 instead examines how an individual preference, if present, would be amplified.
