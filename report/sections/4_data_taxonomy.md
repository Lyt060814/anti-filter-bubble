## 4.2 Which issues carry opposing narratives

**How the taxonomy was built.** Rather than starting from a list of issues we expected to matter, we let the data show which social issues users actually see. We drew 3,000 impressions uniformly from RecIF user histories, so each video appears in proportion to how often it was shown, and asked the reference model (DeepSeek V4 Pro) to label each one from its text description, using a cross-domain prompt (Appendix A.6): the broad domain, a short name for the specific issue, whether the video itself endorses a contested position, and if so which of four narrative types it uses. The prompt applies the common rules in Appendix A.1: reporting or rebutting a claim, telling a conflict story without taking a side, and marketing do not count. 2,973 outputs were valid; the 23 invalid ones are left out of every share. The four types are:

- **Only-right-way**: one choice is presented as the only proper path, and other choices are belittled.
- **Binary opposition**: a question is framed as having exactly two opposed camps or options.
- **Anxiety-raising**: fear, urgency or shame is used to push a view or a choice.
- **Group label**: a whole social group (by gender, region, age and so on) is portrayed negatively, or a single case is presented as typical of the group.

The model writes the issue name as free text, so for domains with at least 30 impressions we grouped the names into sub-issues with fixed keyword rules applied to the model's own labels. For comparison, the same impressions were also labelled by the open model (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507) with an earlier prompt; its figures are shown in the last column of Table 4.1.

**Where opposing narratives appear, and what kind they are.** Most of what users are shown is not about social issues at all: about 7% of impressions (95% interval 6.5%–8.4%) touch a social issue, and the five domains we study account for about 4.5% (3.8%–5.3%). Within those five domains, roughly one impression in six carries an opposing narrative (17%; 12%–24%). Gender and relationships is the largest issue domain, and about one in five of its impressions carries an opposing narrative; about half of these present one choice as the only right one. In body and appearance the opposing narratives we found are all of the only-right-way kind, about appearance standards. Across the whole sample, only-right-way is the most common type (16 of the 39 impressions with an opposing narrative), followed by anxiety-raising (8), group label (6) and binary opposition (5). Group labels are rare: only one fell in gender and relationships, consistent with the rate of roughly 1% that the reference model gives on a larger sample of marriage videos (Section 4.3).

Three cautions apply. First, each domain has only 2 to 54 impressions, so the intervals are wide, often 20 to 40 percentage points; the table shows where opposing narratives concentrate and what kind they are, not precise levels. Second, the cross-domain prompt excluded marketing, and none of the anxiety-raising labels in consumption fell on promotions; with the open model and no such rule, at least 10 of 27 did. Third, the labels come from a model, checked only against other models (Section 4.3).

**The open model overestimates at both steps.** The open model labelled 22% of the same impressions as social-issue content, three times as many as the reference model, and judged almost half of those (47%) to carry an opposing narrative. Of the 660 impressions it placed in a social-issue domain, the reference model placed 450 in non-issue content. On the 210 impressions both models treated as issue content, the open model labelled 69% as opposing and the reference model 17%. This matches the pattern in Section 4.3: the open model reads drama, jokes and everyday family scenes as stances.

**Typical disputes.** The following are illustrative paraphrases of the kind of argument found in each studied domain, written by us from the issue labels; they are not quotations from videos.

- *Gender and relationships*: whether how much a partner spends on dates shows how much they care; how high a bride price is reasonable; whether a woman should keep her own income after marriage. A group-label version: "men from poor rural families bring their whole family into the marriage".
- *Body and appearance*: whether clear skin, a slim body or greater height is needed to be treated well, and whether there is one right standard of beauty.
- *Family and generations*: strict versus relaxed parenting; how much grown children owe their parents; conflict between a wife and her mother-in-law.
- *Life choices and paths*: taking the civil-service exam versus starting work; "lying flat" versus striving.
- *Consumption and money*: saving versus enjoying life now; whether young people should still buy a home and a car.

**Why gender and relationships is the case study.** It is the largest social-issue domain in what users are shown, it carries three of the four narrative types, and its group labels (Appendix A.3) can be defined precisely enough to be labelled and checked. That group labels are rare is itself important for Section 5: a narrative that makes up about 1% of a user's issue videos is hard for the user to notice.

**What we left out, and why.** We exclude three groups of content from the main analysis. (1) *History, national memory and patriotism*, which make up a third of the culture-and-values domain (17 of 51 impressions), and *policing, surveillance and campus control* in public order and freedom. These are politically sensitive: we expect labels to disagree more, and both the ethics of studying users' exposure to them and open discussion of results are harder. (2) *Resources and fairness*, *study and ideas* and *other social issues*, each with 9 or fewer impressions. (3) The rest of culture and values, a broad mix of tradition, celebrity and online culture with no common dispute.

## 4.3 How reliable the labels are

No video was labelled by hand. To estimate label quality we had the open model and a stronger reference model (DeepSeek V4 Pro) label the same 500 marriage-and-family videos independently, sampled in proportion to exposures; a third model (GPT-6.1 Sol) labelled the first 150 as a second reference. With the first version of the prompt, the open model flagged 4.0% of videos as carrying a gender group label and the reference flagged 1.4% (95% interval 0.7%–2.9%). Only two videos were flagged by both. Taking the reference as the standard, the open model's precision was 10% (2/20; 95% interval 3%–30%) and its recall 29% (2/7; 8%–64%). Raw agreement was 95%, but only because group labels are rare; Cohen's kappa was 0.13. Two outputs from the open model were invalid and are reported as missing.

Reading the two models' one-line reasons, most of the open model's false positives fell into three patterns: a family role, such as a mother-in-law or a stepmother, treated as a whole gender (7 of 18); a single character's behaviour in a drama treated as a claim about the group (5); and teasing treated as disparagement (3). We revised the prompt twice in response (Appendix A.4). The first revision moved the two models in opposite directions: the open model flagged half of all videos and the reference model fewer than before. The second revision makes the model answer four yes-or-no questions, each aimed at one of these errors, as separate fields before it gives a label, and rejects outputs whose label does not follow from the answers.

This check has a clear limit: the reference is itself a model, not a human gold standard. Errors shared by both models, for example cues that appear only in the video and not in its description, cannot be detected. The reference flagged only 7 videos, so recall is estimated imprecisely.
